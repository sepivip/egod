How a dirt bike floats

Springs hold you up. Oil decides how fast everything moves. A few clicks on two dials are the difference between a bike that glides and one that throws you off. Scroll down and play with every one of them.

Part 1

Two jobs, one wheel

A modern motocross bike has about 300 mm of suspension travel at each wheel. That is roughly the length of a school ruler, and it does two jobs at the same time.

First, it keeps the tires pressed into the dirt. A tire in the air can't steer, brake or push you forward. Second, it keeps the hits out of your body, so your arms and legs last the whole ride.

Part 2

The spring holds you up

Inside each fork leg, and wrapped around the rear shock, is a steel coil spring. Its stiffness is printed on it in kilograms per millimetre.

A 5.4 kg/mm shock spring needs 5.4 kg to squash it by one millimetre. The rear shock sits in a linkage that multiplies its movement, so the wheel moves about three times further than the shock itself.

When you climb on, the bike sinks into its travel. That sink is called sag, and it is the first thing every suspension tuner measures.

Race sag
Rider on, in full gear, standing. Aim for about 100–105 mm at the rear: a third of the travel. That leaves room to push into holes as well as soak up bumps.
Static sag
The bike alone, on its wheels. Aim for about 30–40 mm. This number tells you whether the spring suits your weight.
Preload
A threaded ring on the shock that squeezes the spring before you sit on it. One full turn is 1.5 mm.

Why preload can't fix the wrong spring

The blue line is how hard the rear spring pushes back at each point of its travel. Where it crosses a load line, the bike stops sinking: that is the sag. Preload slides the line up; a stiffer spring makes it steeper. Try a 105 kg rider: you can wind in preload until race sag looks right, but static sag collapses toward zero. Only a stiffer spring fixes both.

Part 3

Oil controls the speed

A spring on its own would bounce forever, like a pogo stick. The damper is what stops it.

Inside the shock and fork, a piston pushes oil through small holes and stacks of thin steel washers called shims. Oil resists being rushed, so the damper fights speed, not distance. Each end has two main adjusters, counted in clicks out from fully closed:

Compression
How hard the suspension resists being squashed. Firmer holds you up on big landings, but sends small sharp hits straight into your hands.
Rebound
How fast it springs back out. Too fast and the bike bucks like a pogo stick. Too slow and it can't come back up before the next bump arrives.

    Part 5

    The cheat sheet

    Real tuners work in a fixed order, and change one thing at a time.

    1. Springs and sag first. Get race sag near 100 mm with preload, then check static sag. If static sag is off, change the spring, not the preload.
    2. Then compression, for how the bike handles hits.
    3. Then rebound, for how it recovers from them.
    4. Two clicks at a time, on the same piece of track, and write it down.

    The bike is modelled on a modern 450 cc motocross bike: about 300 mm of travel, coil fork springs and a linkage rear shock with a 5.4 kg/mm spring. The physics is a simplified half-bike running live in your browser. It's built for intuition, not for setting up a race bike.
